Isombe

Pounded cassava leaves simmered slowly with aubergine, spinach, peanut butter and palm oil.

  • Cook50 min
  • Prep10 min
  • Serves4
  • LevelEasy
Start cooking
What you need

Ingredients

Change the servings and every quantity updates automatically. Original recipe serves 4.

  • 500 g pounded cassava leaves (fresh or frozen)
  • 4 cups water
  • 1 medium aubergine, diced
  • 2 cups fresh spinach, chopped
  • 1 green pepper, diced
  • 2 medium onions, chopped
  • 3 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 3 tbsp smooth peanut butter (or ground peanuts)
  • 3 tbsp red palm oil
  • 1 stock cube
  • Salt, to taste
The story behind the dish

Culture on a plate

Isombe is one of the most loved home dishes in Rwanda, and close cousins are cooked in Burundi and the Democratic Republic of Congo, where it is called sombe or pondu. It is made from young cassava leaves, which are pounded in a wooden mortar until soft, then cooked for a long time.

The long cooking is important: it softens the leaves and makes them safe and delicious to eat. Families often cook isombe in large quantities for gatherings, and its deep green colour, nutty peanut richness and red palm oil make it a nourishing, plant-based meal that is served alongside plantains, beans or rice.

Method

Cooking instructions

Prepare the leaves

If using fresh cassava leaves, remove the stalks, wash well and pound in a mortar (or pulse in a blender) until finely broken down. Frozen pounded leaves can be used straight from the pack.

Boil the leaves

Bring the water to a boil, add the leaves and simmer uncovered for 30 minutes, stirring occasionally. Never skip this long boil — it is what makes cassava leaves safe to eat.

Add the vegetables

Add the onions, garlic, aubergine and green pepper. Cook for 10 minutes until the aubergine is soft.

Enrich the stew

Loosen the peanut butter with a ladle of the cooking liquid, then stir it in with the palm oil and crumbled stock cube.

Finish with spinach

Add the spinach and simmer for 5–10 minutes more, until the stew is thick and glossy. Season with salt and serve hot.

Cook's tip: Look for frozen pounded cassava leaves (“saka saka” or “pondu”) in African grocery shops — they save most of the preparation time.
